\chapter{Closed three-manifold topology and the Poincare problem}
\label{ch:foundations-v4}
\chaptermark{Closed three-manifold topology}

The Poincare problem asks whether simple connectedness determines a closed
three-manifold. Algebraic topology gives a striking preliminary answer:
such a smooth manifold has the homotopy type of a sphere. A homotopy
equivalence, however, does not provide local coordinates for its inverse,
and does not identify the differentiable structures. The geometric
argument developed in this book is needed to obtain a diffeomorphism.
This chapter establishes the topology available before that argument starts.

\section{Manifolds and the two conclusions}
\label{sec:foundations-endpoints}

\begin{definition}[Manifold conventions]
\label{def:foundations-manifold}
A topological three-manifold is a Hausdorff, second-countable space equipped
with charts onto open subsets of $\mathbb R^3$. In particular, it has no
boundary. A smooth three-manifold has, in addition, smooth transition maps
between its charts. Here smooth means $C^\infty$. A manifold is
\emph{closed} if it is compact and has no boundary.

A space is \emph{simply connected} if it is nonempty and path connected
and any two paths with the same endpoints are homotopic relative to those
endpoints. For a nonempty path-connected space, the last condition is
equivalent to requiring that all based loops be nullhomotopic.
For a point $p\in X$, write $\pi_k(X,p)$ for based homotopy
classes of maps $(I^k,\partial I^k)\to(X,p)$, with their usual group
structure for $k\geq1$.
\end{definition}

The standard sphere throughout the book is
\[
 S^3=\{x\in\mathbb R^4:\|x\|=1\},
\]
with its subspace topology and standard smooth hypersurface structure.
The two conclusions to be proved have different hypotheses:
\begin{theorem}[Smooth and topological Poincare conclusions]
\label{thm:foundations-targets}
Every closed, simply connected smooth three-manifold is diffeomorphic to
$S^3$. Every closed, simply connected topological three-manifold is
homeomorphic to $S^3$.
\source{MT2007}\dcref{Corollary 0.2(a), p. xii, for the smooth conclusion}
\end{theorem}

This is the target of the book, not a theorem established in this chapter.
The quantifier is over every manifold with the stated structure; neither
conclusion assumes an orientation, a Riemannian metric, or a chosen sphere
identification. The smooth conclusion is the conclusion of
\cite[Corollary 0.2(a), p. xii]{MT2007}. Passage from the smooth to the
topological conclusion requires a separate compatible-smoothing theorem.

Unless stated otherwise, $M$ below is a closed, simply connected smooth
three-manifold with the separation and countability hypotheses of
Definition~\ref{def:foundations-manifold}. Homology and cohomology have
integer coefficients. We write $H_j(X,A)$ for relative singular homology
and $H_c^j(X)$ for singular cohomology with compact supports. A homotopy
equivalence is denoted by $\simeq$, a homeomorphism by $\cong$, and a smooth
identification explicitly by ``diffeomorphic.''

\begin{proposition}[Initial topology]
\label{prop:foundations-initial}
The chosen smooth atlas of $M$ admits compatible orientation signs, and
$M$ admits a finite CW structure of dimension at most three. For every
$p\in M$,
\[
 \pi_1(M,p)=0,\qquad \pi_2(M,p)=0,\qquad
 \pi_3(M,p)\cong\mathbb Z.
\]
Moreover, $M\simeq S^3$.
\uses{def:foundations-manifold}
\source{MT2007}\dcref{discussion after Proposition 18.9, p. 424; after Claim 18.20, p. 431}
\end{proposition}

We prove the separate assertions below. In particular, the last assertion
is a homotopy conclusion and is never used as a sphere recognition theorem.
The nonzero third homotopy class and the orientation obstruction are the
parts required when the geometric proof begins. The cited discussions in
\cite{MT2007} explain their role; the constructions here also account for
the finite CW and homotopy-equivalence assertions.

\section{A finite three-dimensional CW structure}
\label{sec:foundations-triangulation}

A CW structure on $M$ means cells with characteristic maps into $M$ whose
open-cell images partition it, whose boundaries lie in lower skeleta, and
whose weak topology is the given topology. We need an actual such
structure on $M$, rather than merely a space with the same homotopy type.
The following construction gives more: a homeomorphism from a finite
simplicial complex of dimension at most three.

\begin{proposition}[Finite triangulated model]
\label{prop:foundations-triangulation}
Every nonempty compact smooth three-manifold that is Hausdorff and
second countable is homeomorphic to the realization of the order complex
of a finite partially ordered set. Each chain in this partially ordered
set has at most four elements.
\end{proposition}

Here the order complex has one vertex for each element of the partially
ordered set and one simplex for each nonempty finite chain. The following
proof describes the geometric construction, including the estimates that
turn a nearby polyhedron into a homeomorphic model.
The smooth embedding, graph charts, and tubular neighborhood are
ordinary differential topology; their constructions are recalled below.
The quantitative avoidance, section, and flag estimates are developed
explicitly, rather than deduced from a general triangulation theorem.

\begin{proof}
Start with a smooth embedding $e:M\to\mathbb R^N$, enlarging the ambient
space if necessary so that $N>3$. Such an embedding is obtained from a
finite chart cover and smooth cutoff functions: use the functions and
their products with chart coordinates as Euclidean coordinates. Choose
the cutoffs so that some cutoff is nonzero near every point. Equality of
all coordinates then identifies two points in a common chart, and the
same coordinates give injectivity of the derivative. Compactness makes
the resulting injective immersion a closed embedding.

Put $L=N+1$ and identify $T_pM$ with its image under $D e_p$ in
$\mathbb R^N$. Uniform graph charts for this compact embedding write $e(M)$
near $e(p)$ as
\[
 e(p)+v+r_p(v),\qquad v\in T_pM,
 \qquad r_p(v)\in(T_pM)^\perp,
 \qquad \operatorname{Lip}(r_p)\leq\epsilon.
\]
For every prescribed $\epsilon>0$, compactness allows a common positive
chart radius. There is also a positive tubular radius $r$ on which the
nearest-point projection $\nu$ to $e(M)$ is defined and continuous, and
its fibers are normal to $M$. To obtain a uniform neighborhood, apply
the inverse function theorem to the normal map $(p,v)\mapsto e(p)+v$
along the zero section. Compactness supplies a common radius. If
injectivity failed at every smaller radius, two sequences of normal
representations would converge to the same point of the zero section,
contradicting its local inverse. A distance minimizer exists by compactness;
first variation makes its displacement normal, and uniqueness in this
neighborhood identifies it with the normal-map inverse.

Triangulate a sufficiently large ambient box by the usual grid simplices
of mesh $h$, and perturb its vertices slightly. The perturbation is
chosen to keep every face of dimension at most $N-4$ a positive distance
from $e(M)$. The quantitative form used here gives a constant $b>0$
depending only on $N$ such that these faces have distance greater than
$bh$. At the same time every simplex has diameter at most $2Lh$ and
every altitude is at least $h/4$.

Here is the finite avoidance argument. For $m$ proper affine planes and
a movement ball of radius $R$ centered at $c$, consider the $(m+1)^N$
points with coordinates
\[
 x(a)_i=c_i+\frac{R}{N+1}
       \left(\frac{2a_i+1}{m+1}-1\right),\qquad 0\leq a_i\leq m.
\]
Their coordinate absolute displacements sum to less than $R$, so they
lie in the ball. For each plane choose a containing hyperplane
$\langle u,x\rangle=d$ with $\|u\|=1$. Some coordinate of $u$ has
absolute value at least $1/(N+1)$. Holding the other coordinates fixed,
successive candidate values of $\langle u,x\rangle$ differ by at least
$2R/((N+1)^2(m+1))$. Thus at most one lies in a slab of half-width
\[
 t=\frac{R}{4(N+1)^2(m+1)}.
\]
Each slab contains at most $(m+1)^{N-1}$ candidates. Their union contains
at most $m(m+1)^{N-1}<(m+1)^N$, leaving a point with distance greater
than $t$ from every plane.

Apply this at successive grid vertices. A bound for the number of faces
incident to a vertex is $J_N=2^{2N+2}(N!+1)$. For each low-dimensional
face completed at the current vertex, the forbidden affine plane contains
a nearby tangent three-plane and the already chosen vertices of that face.
It is proper: a face with $k$ vertices under consideration has $k+3\leq N$,
so this span has dimension at most $3+(k-1)<N$.
Choose the movement radius $a_Nh$, where
\[
 a_N=\frac1{16L},\qquad
 s_N=\frac1{4L^2(J_N+1)},\qquad A_N=a_Ns_N.
\]
The avoidance calculation gives clearance $A_Nh$ from all these planes.

The clearance propagates from an old face to its join with the new vertex.
Indeed, let an old face be at distance at least $d$ from a plane $P$,
let $P^*$ contain both the old face and $P$, and let the new vertex $z$
have distance at least $t$ from $P^*$. Suppose its distance from any point
of the old face is at most $D$. At $(1-a)y+az$ the distance from $P$ is
at least both $at$ and $d-aD$. Taking the larger lower bound gives
$dt/(D+t)$. Use this with $D=2Lh$ and with the previous face's clearance.
The graph error can be bounded by $b h/100$, where
\[
 b_j=\frac{A_N}{2}\left(\frac{A_N}{8L}\right)^j,
 \qquad b=b_{N-4}.
\]
The join inequality, with this allowance for graph error, gives clearance
$b_jh$ for a completed face of dimension $j\leq N-4$. This proves the
claimed separation by induction over processed vertices and face dimension.
Vertices far from the manifold need no change. The mesh is chosen within
the uniform chart radius so that all faces involved in a step lie in the
same graph chart.
The piecewise affine displacement has Lipschitz constant at most $1/4$;
thus the perturbed grid is still a triangulation. Indeed, a map $x+u(x)$
with $\operatorname{Lip}(u)<1$ is injective, and solving $x=y-u(x)$ gives
its inverse by contraction. This also retains the diameter and altitude
bounds after slightly weakening their constants.

Call an ambient face \emph{active} if its convex hull meets $e(M)$.
An active face has between $N-2$ and $N+1$ vertices. We next show that
minimal active faces have $N-2$ vertices, meet the manifold in one point,
and occur through every vertex of an active face.

Fix a nearby $p$ and let $A(x)=P_{(T_pM)^\perp}(x-e(p))$, whose target
has dimension $d=N-3$. Write $Q$ for tangent projection. In a simplex
within distance $R=6Lh$ of $e(p)$, the graph chart gives
\[
 \operatorname{dist}(x,e(M))\leq\|A(x)\|+\epsilon R,
 \qquad \|A(e(q))\|\leq\epsilon R.
\]
Take $a=bh/2$. For sufficiently small $\epsilon$, the grid separation
therefore implies $\|A(x)\|\geq a$ on every face with at most $d$
vertices, while $\epsilon R<a$.

Let $P$ be the convex hull of the normal projections of all vertices of
a face. Every boundary point of $P$ lies in the convex hull of at most
$d$ projected vertices: a supporting hyperplane reduces the dimension
by one, and an affinely dependent convex combination can be reduced by
varying its coefficients until one becomes zero. Repeating this leaves
at most $d$ terms. If $P$ contained a point of norm less than $a$ but
did not contain zero, its closest point to zero would be such a boundary
point, contradicting the small-face gap. The same boundary argument
shows that, once $0\in P$, the entire closed ball of radius $a$ is in
$P$.

For a prescribed vertex $v$, follow the ray opposite $A(v)$ from zero
until it first meets the boundary of $P$. The boundary point is a
convex combination of at most $d$ projected vertices. Combining it
with $A(v)$ gives zero as a convex combination on a face containing $v$
with at most $d+1$ vertices. The gap rules out $d$ or fewer, so the
face has exactly $d+1=N-2$ vertices. Its projected vertices are
affinely independent: otherwise the same coefficient reduction would
give a forbidden zero combination with at most $d$ terms.

On such a minimal face, $A$ is an affine isomorphism onto its affine
image, which is all of the normal space. Let $x_0$ be its unique zero
and write its inverse as $n\mapsto x_0+Bn$. The ball inclusion just
proved gives
\[
 \|B\|\leq\frac{\operatorname{diam}(s)}a:
 \qquad \|n\|\leq a\ \Longrightarrow\
 x_0+Bn\in\operatorname{conv}(s),\quad
 \|Bn\|\leq\operatorname{diam}(s).
\]
A point of this face is on the manifold graph precisely when
\[
 n=r_p\bigl(Q(x_0+Bn-e(p))\bigr).
\]
The right side sends the normal ball of radius $\epsilon R$ into itself
and has Lipschitz constant at most
$\epsilon\operatorname{diam}(s)/a<1$. Contraction gives a unique graph
intersection, with displacement from $x_0$ at most
$\operatorname{diam}(s)\epsilon R/a$.
This uniqueness holds in the entire face: any other graph intersection
has normal coordinate of norm at most $\epsilon R$, and hence is a
fixed point of the same contraction. The graph chart contains every
manifold point in this face, by the uniform chart-radius choice.

If a face meets the manifold, its projected hull contains a point of
norm at most $\epsilon R<a$, hence contains zero by the boundary
argument. Conversely, if it meets the tangent plane, the minimal-face
construction and contraction give a manifold intersection in a subface.
This proves equality of the two sets of active faces and their incidence
relations, as well as the assertion through each prescribed vertex.

For each active face $s$, average the intersection points associated to
its minimal active subfaces, obtaining a point $c_s$ in its convex hull.
These choices are made once per face, so they agree when a face occurs
in different simplices. If $w_v$ are the barycentric coordinates of
$c_s$ in $s$, separation from lower-dimensional faces and the diameter
bound give
\[
 w_v\geq \frac{b}{2L\,2^{N+1}}\quad(v\in s).
\]
To see the source of this bound, a minimal-section point cannot be closer
than $bh$ to the opposite face, whereas the entire simplex has diameter
at most $2Lh$. For each vertex at least one averaged point therefore
has that vertex coordinate at least $b/(2L)$; there are at most
$2^{N+1}$ terms in the average.

Order the active faces by inclusion and form their order complex $C$.
Strictly increasing cardinalities in the range $N-2,\ldots,N+1$ show
that a chain has at most four elements. Send its vertex $s$ to $c_s$
and extend affinely on each simplex. Denote the resulting map by
$F:|C|\to\mathbb R^N$.

The positivity of the center coordinates makes $F$ injective. Here is
the coordinate argument, which will also apply to the tangent centers.
For a convex combination of centers along a flag, discard zero
coefficients and let $s$ be its largest face. Its ambient barycentric
support is exactly $s$. Two equal image points therefore have the same
largest face, since relative interiors of ambient simplices are
disjoint. If $z_v$ are their common barycentric coordinates and $c_v>0$
those of $c_s$, the coefficient of this largest center is
\[
 \min_{v\in s}\frac{z_v}{c_v}.
\]
Indeed all smaller centers contribute nonnegative coordinates, and some
vertex of $s$ is absent from their largest support. Subtract this
coefficient times $c_s$ and repeat on the remaining support. The
procedure recovers every nonzero flag coefficient, proving injectivity.

For the tangent plane the active section of an ambient face is a
convex polytope, described by nonnegative barycentric coordinates,
their sum being one, and the equation $A(x)=0$. Its vertices are
exactly the points in minimal active faces. In fact a section point
with more than $d+1$ positive coordinates admits a nonzero variation
preserving both the sum and $A(x)$; small variations of either sign
stay in the section, so this point is not extreme. A minimal active
face instead has a unique section point. Averaging these vertices
gives a tangent center with positive coordinates on the entire face,
by the prescribed-vertex incidence argument above.

We spell out why these centers give a subdivision. Let $z$ be a
section point, let $s$ be its positive coordinate support, and let $c$
be the tangent center indexed by $s$. If $z=c$ there is nothing to
prove. Otherwise set
\[
 a=\min_{v\in s}\frac{z_v}{c_v},\qquad
 y=\frac{z-ac}{1-a}.
\]
Then $0<a<1$, and $y$ is another section point with strictly smaller
support: its coordinates are nonnegative and sum to one, its affine
constraint is zero, and at least one coordinate vanishes. Induction
expresses $y$ in a flag of smaller active faces, and
$z=ac+(1-a)y$ adds $s$ to that flag. The preceding uniqueness argument
shows that two resulting simplices meet only in their common faces.
This is the usual coning of the subdivided boundary to an interior
center, here with the prescribed centers and supports. The finite
realization is compact, so its continuous bijection to the section
is a homeomorphism. Choices on common ambient faces agree, and the
subdivisions paste.

In particular these local flags cover the closed tangent disk of radius
$4Lh$. A point of this disk lies in an ambient simplex. Every point
of that simplex is within $4Lh+2Lh=6Lh$ of $e(p)$, so the local
incidence argument applies to the whole simplex and all its faces.
Its tangent section therefore supplies the required flag representation.

It remains to prove that $\nu F$ identifies $|C|$ with $e(M)$.
This conclusion is not obtained merely by estimating the distance from
$F(|C|)$ to $M$. Compare $F$, near $p$, with the flag realization of
the affine tangent-plane sections. The latter fills a disk in
$e(p)+T_pM$. The comparison has the form
\[
 v\longmapsto e(p)+v+E_p(v)
 \quad(\|v\|\leq4Lh),
 \qquad \|E_p(v)\|\leq Lh.
\]
The lower bounds on altitudes and barycentric coordinates control the
inverse affine maps on successive flags. More precisely, put
\[
 \eta=\frac{b}{4L\,2^{N+1}},\qquad
 C_N=\frac{96L^2}{b}\bigl((1+\eta^{-1})^4-1\bigr).
\]
The minimal-face displacement bound, averaged over the same faces on
the graph and tangent sides, is at most
\[
 \frac{2Lh}{bh/2}\,\epsilon(6Lh)
       =\frac{24L^2\epsilon h}{b}=:\delta.
\]
We give the coordinate estimate responsible for the power of four.
For a flag $s_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq s_k$, extend each tangent
center's barycentric coordinate vector $c_i$ by zero to the largest
simplex. Its support is exactly $s_i$, every nonzero coordinate is at
least $\eta$, and $\|c_i\|_\infty\leq1$.
For arbitrary real $w_i$ put $z=\sum_iw_ic_i$.
The largest support has a coordinate absent from all the smaller ones.
At this coordinate, $z_v=w_k(c_k)_v$, so
$|w_k|\leq\eta^{-1}\|z\|_\infty$.
Subtracting $w_kc_k$ leaves a vector of norm at most
$(1+\eta^{-1})\|z\|_\infty$. Induction on $k$, starting from the
empty flag, yields
\[
 \sum_i|w_i|\leq
       \bigl((1+\eta^{-1})^k-1\bigr)\|z\|_\infty.
\]
For a difference of two points in the same flag simplex, $\sum_iw_i=0$.
If $y$ is the corresponding ambient difference, the altitude bound gives
$\|z\|_\infty\leq\|y\|/(h/4)$: each affine barycentric coordinate
has derivative of norm the reciprocal of its vertex altitude.
The change in displacement is bounded by $\delta\sum_i|w_i|$.
All flags have $k\leq4$, so these inequalities prove the same Lipschitz
bound on each flag simplex. A segment in the tangent disk crosses only
finitely many simplices; partitioning the segment and summing its lengths
extends that bound to the whole disk. Thus
\[
 \operatorname{Lip}(E_p)
 \leq\frac{24L^2\epsilon h/b}{h/4}
          \bigl((1+\eta^{-1})^4-1\bigr)
 = C_N\epsilon.
\]
Choose
\[
 0<\epsilon\leq
 \min\left\{\frac{b}{1000L},\frac{1}{2(C_N+1)}\right\},
 \qquad 0<h<\frac r{10L},
\]
and also take $h$ within the graph-chart bound. Thus the last Lipschitz
constant is at most $1/2$.

Let $P_p$ be orthogonal projection onto $T_pM$. A point of this local
flag realization lies on the normal fiber over $p$ precisely when
\[
 v=-P_pE_p(v).
\]
The right-hand side maps the closed ball of radius $Lh$ into itself and
is a contraction. There is exactly one solution there, and the same
Lipschitz estimate implies uniqueness throughout the disk of radius
$4Lh$. The resulting point lies within $2Lh<r$ of $e(p)$, so its nearest
point is $e(p)$.

Every point of $F(|C|)$ lies in an active ambient simplex, hence is within
$2Lh$ of the manifold. To check that the local comparison captures
every point in the normal fiber over $p$, write such a point as
$y=\sum_i\lambda_i c_{s_i}$ along a flag with largest face $s$.
Both $y$ and the centers lie in that ambient simplex. Since
$\|y-e(p)\|\leq2Lh$, its diameter bound puts the entire simplex
within $4Lh$ of $e(p)$. Replace each $c_{s_i}$ by its tangent center
and retain the same coefficients $\lambda_i$. This gives a tangent
point $x$ in the same simplex, thus in the disk of radius $4Lh$,
whose local comparison image is exactly $y$.

Normal-fiber uniqueness now shows that two points with the same nearest
point have the same image under $F$; the injectivity of $F$ proved above
shows that they were the same point of $|C|$. The constructed solution
over each $p$ proves surjectivity. Consequently $\nu F$ is a continuous
bijection from the compact finite complex $|C|$ to the Hausdorff space
$e(M)$, and hence is a homeomorphism. Transporting the simplicial cell
structure along $e^{-1}\nu F$ proves the assertion.
\end{proof}

The separation and flag-coordinate estimates in this construction concern
the embedded manifold; they do not use simple connectedness or any form of
the Poincare conclusion. Simple connectedness enters the next arguments.

\section{Two orientation constructions}
\label{sec:foundations-orientation}

We use orientation in two forms. Signed smooth charts supply the
obstruction needed for surgery. Coherent integral local homology
generators supply the fundamental class and cap maps. Both can be
constructed directly from simple connectedness.

\begin{definition}[Compatible chart signs]
\label{def:foundations-chart-signs}
For each chart $x_i:U_i\to\mathbb R^3$, choose a locally constant
function $\varepsilon_i:U_i\to\{1,-1\}$. The signs are compatible if
at every $p\in U_i\cap U_j$,
\[
 \varepsilon_i(p)\varepsilon_j(p)
 \det D(x_j\circ x_i^{-1})_{x_i(p)}>0.
\]
Allowing signs to be locally constant accommodates disconnected chart
domains and overlaps.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[The determinant orientation cover]
\label{lem:foundations-determinant-cover}
A simply connected smooth three-manifold admits compatible chart signs.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The coordinate changes of the tangent bundle have nonzero determinants
$d_{ij}$. The chain rule gives $d_{ii}=1$ and
$d_{ik}=d_{jk}d_{ij}$. Their signs are locally constant. Glue
$U_i\times\{1,-1\}$ by
$(p,a)_i\sim(p,a\operatorname{sgn}d_{ij}(p))_j$.
The cocycle identities give a two-sheeted covering.

Choose $p_0\in M$ and one point of the fiber above it. The identity of
$M$ lifts to this cover because its fundamental group is trivial;
manifolds are locally path connected, so the usual covering lifting
criterion applies \cite[Proposition 1.33, p. 61]{Hatcher}. The lift is a
continuous section. Its coordinates give locally constant signs with
$\varepsilon_j=\varepsilon_i\operatorname{sgn}d_{ij}$, which is exactly
the required positivity.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Local integral orientation]
\label{def:foundations-local-orientation}
For $p\in M$ the local homology group is
$L_p=H_3(M,M\setminus\{p\})$. An integral local orientation is a
choice of generator $\omega_p\in L_p$ for every $p$ that is locally
represented: around each point there is an open set $U$ and a class in
$H_3(M,M\setminus U)$ whose restrictions to the groups $L_q$, $q\in U$,
are the chosen generators.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Coherent generators]
\label{lem:foundations-coherent}
A simply connected topological three-manifold admits a local integral
orientation. No smooth structure is needed for this assertion.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a closed coordinate ball $K$ inside a chart and let $U$ be its
interior. Excision and the Euclidean pair calculation identify
$H_3(M,M\setminus K)$ with $\mathbb Z$. For every $q\in U$, restriction
to $L_q$ is an isomorphism. One way to calculate this is to use the
boundary isomorphism for the punctured Euclidean space and then its radial
deformation retraction to $S^2$; moving the puncture within the interior
does not change the relative generator.

These coordinate balls give local frames for the groups $L_q$. Two
frames differ by an automorphism of $\mathbb Z$, thus by $1$ or $-1$.
The sign is locally constant: if two relative classes have the same
restriction at one point, their difference restricts to zero on a
sufficiently small neighborhood of that point. This localization
fact follows by representing a zero class with a finite bounding chain
and shrinking the neighborhood away from the support of the relative
chain left outside the puncture.

Glue the two possible generators using these locally constant transition
signs. This is again a two-sheeted covering. Lift the identity after
choosing a generator over one point, as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:foundations-determinant-cover}. In a frame the section is
locally a constant sign, so the corresponding signed coordinate-ball
class represents it on a neighborhood. These are the required
$\omega_q$.
\end{proof}

The elementary local homology and orientation facts used here are those
of \cite[Section 3.3, pp. 233--235]{Hatcher}. The two covering
constructions need not choose the same sign at the initial point. All
uses below are invariant under changing the global sign.

\section{The third homology group}
\label{sec:foundations-top-homology}

We recall the compact-support form of the local-to-global argument. Put
$H_j^K(M)=H_j(M,M\setminus K)$ for a compact set $K$. Restriction from
$H_3^K(M)$ to $L_p$, $p\in K$, detects a class: a class with zero
restriction at every point of $K$ is zero. Moreover, a locally
represented orientation glues to a class in $H_3^K(M)$ with the
prescribed restrictions.

For completeness, the compact-support argument proceeds as follows.
In a Euclidean chart, a compact convex support has the same relative
homology as a point, by radial retraction of its complement. The
relative Mayer--Vietoris sequence extends both detection in degree three
and vanishing above degree three from two compact supports and their
intersection to their union. Thus finite unions of small closed balls
have these properties. For a general compact support $K$ in the chart, a relative
cycle has boundary supported in a compact subset of its complement.
Choose a finite union $B$ of small closed balls centered at points of
$K$, covering $K$ and disjoint from this boundary support. The cycle
is already a relative cycle for $B$, proving high-degree vanishing
for $K$ from that for $B$. For detection, suppose its point restrictions
vanish on $K$. On each ball, restriction to the local group at its
center is an isomorphism, so the class restricts to zero on that ball.
Detection for the finite union gives zero on $B$ and hence on $K$.

To pass to the manifold, cover a compact support by finitely many compact
pieces contained in charts. When adding one piece, its intersection
with the preceding union is a union of fewer chart-contained compact
pieces, so the same induction applies to the intersection. Local
orientation representatives glue using the exact segment
\[
 0\longrightarrow H_3^{K\cup L}(M)
 \longrightarrow H_3^K(M)\oplus H_3^L(M)
 \longrightarrow H_3^{K\cap L}(M).
\]
The last arrow can be taken to be the difference of restrictions;
the zero on the left comes from $H_4^{K\cap L}(M)=0$.
Detection makes the local representatives agree on intersections;
exactness supplies the glued class and its uniqueness. These are the
ordinary compact-support orientation facts in
\cite[Lemma 3.27, pp. 236--238]{Hatcher}.

\begin{proposition}[Top integral homology]
\label{prop:foundations-top-homology}
If $M$ is a closed, connected topological three-manifold with a local
integral orientation $\omega$, there is a unique class $[M]$ restricting
to $\omega_p$ at every point, and
\[
 H_3(M)\longrightarrow\mathbb Z,\qquad z[M]\longmapsto z
\]
is an isomorphism.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Apply compact-support gluing with $K=M$. The resulting class belongs to
$H_3(M,M\setminus M)=H_3(M)$ and has the stated restrictions.
Support detection gives uniqueness.

For any $a\in H_3(M)$, define the integer $c_a(p)$ by
$a|_p=c_a(p)\omega_p$. This integer exists and is unique since
$\omega_p$ is a generator of the infinite cyclic group $L_p$.
It is locally constant. Indeed, take a neighborhood representative
$b$ of $\omega$; restrict $a$ to the same relative group as $b$.
The local restriction of $a-c_a(p)b$ at $p$ is then zero.
Localization shrinks the neighborhood until all its restrictions vanish.
Thus $c_a(q)=c_a(p)$ there.

Connectedness makes $c_a$ constant. Fix $p_0$. The map
$a\mapsto c_a(p_0)$ is a homomorphism and is injective, since a class
with coefficient zero has every point restriction zero. It is
surjective since $[M]$ has coefficient $1$. Its inverse is
$z\mapsto z[M]$.
\end{proof}

This proof identifies the generator using its local restrictions; it
does not assume a triangulated fundamental cycle in advance. It is the
oriented case of \cite[Theorem 3.26, p. 236]{Hatcher}.

\section{Cap products and vanishing second homology}
\label{sec:foundations-second-homology}

For an open subset $U\subset M$, the orientation restricts to $U$.
Define
\[
 H_c^q(U)=\mathop{\mathrm{colim}}_{K\subset U\text{ compact}}
                H^q(U,U\setminus K).
\]
Restriction of the orientation to a compact support, followed by cap
product, defines
\[
 D_U^q:H_c^q(U)\longrightarrow H_{3-q}(U)\qquad(0\leq q\leq3).
\]
At the chain level one evaluates the cochain on the front face of an
oriented simplex and retains the complementary face. The cap boundary
formula ensures that this descends to homology. Excision and uniqueness
of the supported orientation classes make the map independent of the
chosen compact support and compatible with extension from an open
subset. These are the compact-support cap maps of
\cite[Section 3.3, pp. 239--245]{Hatcher}.

Only a particular part of duality is needed. Retain all four assertions
in the following property of an open set $U$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:foundations-cap-property}
\begin{split}
 &D_U^1\text{ is surjective},\\
 &D_U^2\text{ and }D_U^3\text{ are isomorphisms},\\
 &H_c^q(U)=0\quad\text{for every }q\geq4.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The higher-degree clauses are needed to maintain the first clause under
unions. Establishing only surjectivity in degree one would not close
the induction.

\begin{lemma}[Union step]
\label{lem:foundations-cap-union}
If $U,V$, and $U\cap V$ satisfy
\eqref{eq:foundations-cap-property}, then so does $U\cup V$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $J=U\cap V$ and $W=U\cup V$. Compare the compact-support
cohomology Mayer--Vietoris sequence with the homology sequence by cap
product. Around degree one the rows are
\[
\begin{split}
 H_c^1(U)\oplus H_c^1(V)&\longrightarrow H_c^1(W)
 \longrightarrow H_c^2(J)\longrightarrow H_c^2(U)\oplus H_c^2(V),\\
 H_2(U)\oplus H_2(V)&\longrightarrow H_2(W)
 \longrightarrow H_1(J)\longrightarrow H_1(U)\oplus H_1(V).
\end{split}
\]
The vertical maps are the corresponding $D^q$. Sum and difference
maps commute with cap product; the connecting square has the usual
degree sign, which can be absorbed into one connecting map without
changing exactness.

Given $z\in H_2(W)$, lift its boundary in $H_1(J)$ by the isomorphism
$D_J^2$ to $v\in H_c^2(J)$. Its image in
$H_c^2(U)\oplus H_c^2(V)$ is zero because the corresponding homology
image is zero and $D_U^2,D_V^2$ are injective. Exactness lifts $v$ to
$\beta\in H_c^1(W)$. Now $z-D_W^1\beta$ has zero boundary. It comes
from $H_2(U)\oplus H_2(V)$, where the two degree-one cap maps are
surjective. Adding the images of their lifts to $\beta$ gives a
preimage of $z$.

The next five-term segment proves that $D_W^2$ is an isomorphism:
its four neighboring vertical maps are $D_J^2$, $D_U^2\oplus D_V^2$,
$D_J^3$, and $D_U^3\oplus D_V^3$, all isomorphisms. For degree three
the two exact sequences end in $H_0(W)\to0$ and
$H_c^3(W)\to H_c^4(J)=0$; comparison of cokernels gives the
isomorphism $D_W^3$. Finally the segment
$H_c^q(U)\oplus H_c^q(V)\to H_c^q(W)\to H_c^{q+1}(J)$
has zero outer terms for $q\geq4$. This proves all four clauses.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The property on chart domains]
\label{lem:foundations-chart-cap}
Every open subset of a coordinate chart satisfies
\eqref{eq:foundations-cap-property}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In an open rectangular box in $\mathbb R^3$, ordinary homology vanishes
in positive degrees, while compact-support cohomology is $\mathbb Z$
in degree three and zero in the other degrees. This follows by excision
from the cube modulo its boundary. Capping its relative top generator
with the dual top cochain gives a point with coefficient $1$.
Consequently $D^3$ is an isomorphism; the other required maps are maps
between zero groups. Changing the local orientation multiplies cap by
$-1$, preserving the assertions.

The intersection of two axis-parallel open boxes is another such box
or is empty. The union lemma therefore proves the property for finite
unions of boxes. More explicitly, when adding one box, its intersection
with the previous union is a union of fewer box intersections, so the
same induction applies to the intersection term.

Every open subset of $\mathbb R^3$ is the directed union of its finite
unions of relatively contained boxes. A compactly supported cohomology
class and any finite singular cycle or bounding chain are captured at
some stage. Thus these groups and cap maps commute with the relevant
directed limits. Surjectivity, injectivity, and the stated vanishings
pass to the union. Transport through a chart proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Vanishing second homology]
\label{prop:foundations-second-homology}
A closed simply connected smooth three-manifold satisfies $H_2(M)=0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose the local orientation of
Lemma~\ref{lem:foundations-coherent} and a finite chart cover
$U_1,\ldots,U_m$ of $M$. Inductively prove the cap property for
\emph{every} open set $W\subset U_1\cup\cdots\cup U_k$.
For the successor step write
\[
 W=(W\cap U_{k+1})\cup
       \bigl(W\cap(U_1\cup\cdots\cup U_k)\bigr).
\]
The first set and the intersection lie in $U_{k+1}$, so the chart
lemma applies. The second set satisfies the induction hypothesis.
Lemma~\ref{lem:foundations-cap-union} now gives the assertion for $W$.
At $W=M$, it yields a surjection $H_c^1(M)\to H_2(M)$.

Since $M$ is compact, $H_c^1(M)=H^1(M)$. We show directly that this
group vanishes. Let $\phi$ be an integral singular one-cocycle. Write
$I(\gamma)$ for its value on a path regarded as a singular edge.
The cocycle identity on an affine triangle gives
$I(\gamma\delta)=I(\gamma)+I(\delta)$ and gives zero on constant
paths. Applied to the two triangles in a homotopy square, it gives
invariance under homotopy relative to endpoints: the diagonal terms
cancel and the two endpoint edges are constant.

Fix $p_0\in M$, choose a path $\gamma_x$ from $p_0$ to each $x$, and
put $a(x)=I(\gamma_x)$. Simple connectedness makes any path from
$x$ to $y$ homotopic relative to endpoints to
$\gamma_x^{-1}\gamma_y$. Hence its value is $a(y)-a(x)$.
The zero-cochain $a$ therefore has coboundary $\phi$. It follows that
$H^1(M)=0$ and that the surjective cap map has zero domain. Thus
$H_2(M)=0$.
\end{proof}

The chart-cover and cocycle arguments are topological. The finite
triangulated model from Section~\ref{sec:foundations-triangulation}
also permits them to be performed on one small, finite-complex carrier
and transported to $M$. This change of carrier introduces no additional
geometric assertion and does not weaken the manifold hypotheses.

\section{From homology to based homotopy}
\label{sec:foundations-hurewicz}

We use the integral Hurewicz theorem in its first nonzero degree: if $X$
is path connected and $\pi_i(X,p)=0$ for $1\leq i<n$, where $n\geq2$,
then
\[
 h_n:\pi_n(X,p)\longrightarrow H_n(X)
\]
is an isomorphism. Here $h_n$ sends a based sphere map to the image of
the oriented sphere generator. This is
\cite[Theorem 4.32, p. 366]{Hatcher}; it is general algebraic topology,
not a three-manifold recognition result.

It is useful to specify the mechanism behind the maps used here.
The vanishing lower homotopy groups let one straighten singular simplices
inductively so that their low-dimensional faces are constant at $p$,
while keeping the choices coherent on common faces. The homotopies
produce prism chains, so a cycle and its straightening represent the
same homology class. A straightened $n$-simplex with constant boundary
is a based sphere map. A cycle is a finite integral sum of such
simplices, and addition in $\pi_n$ realizes that sum. This gives
surjectivity. For injectivity, carry out the coherent straightening
also on a bounding chain in degree $n+1$, keeping already based faces
fixed. The face cancellations provide the relative homotopy between
the boundary sphere maps. Thus homologous images have the same based
homotopy class. In the two applications below, these are statements
about degrees two and three with all their connectivity hypotheses
verified explicitly.

\begin{corollary}[The initial based classes]
\label{cor:foundations-homotopy-groups}
For every $p\in M$, $\pi_2(M,p)=0$ and $\pi_3(M,p)\cong\mathbb Z$.
In particular there exists a nonzero element of $\pi_3(M,p)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Simple connectedness gives $\pi_1(M,p)=0$. Hurewicz in degree two
identifies $\pi_2(M,p)$ with $H_2(M)=0$. Its degree-three version now
applies, and Proposition~\ref{prop:foundations-top-homology} identifies
$H_3(M)$ with $\mathbb Z$. The class corresponding to $1$ is nonzero.
\end{proof}

A chosen orientation determines the last isomorphism; reversing it changes
the generator's sign. No preferred sign is needed for the nontriviality
argument. Changing basepoint uses the isomorphism induced by a path.
The conclusion holds at every point, but any comparison of specific
based classes must still specify the identifications being used.

\section{The stronger sphere homotopy equivalence}
\label{sec:foundations-cw-equivalence}

The finite CW structure gives an explicit proof of the last assertion
of Proposition~\ref{prop:foundations-initial}. The proof is a
three-dimensional cellular construction; it does not require checking
an isomorphism on every homotopy group.

\begin{proposition}[A three-dimensional cellular recognition result]
\label{prop:foundations-cw-equivalence}
Let $C$ be a nonempty path-connected CW complex of dimension at most
three, with $\pi_1(C,p)=\pi_2(C,p)=0$ for every $p\in C$ and
$\pi_3(C,p)\cong\mathbb Z$. Then $C\simeq S^3$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose a zero-cell $p$. A nonempty CW complex has such a cell: otherwise
take a cell of least occupied dimension; a positive-dimensional cell
has nonempty boundary mapping into the lower skeleton, a contradiction.
The sphere has $\pi_1(S^3)=\pi_2(S^3)=0$ and $H_3(S^3)=\mathbb Z$;
Hurewicz therefore identifies $\pi_3(S^3)$ with $\mathbb Z$.
Represent a generator of $\pi_3(C,p)$ by a based map $f:S^3\to C$.
Naturality of the degree-three Hurewicz homomorphism shows that
$f_*:\pi_3(S^3)\to\pi_3(C,p)$ is an isomorphism: a generator maps
to the selected generator.

Let $A=C^{(2)}$. Its inclusion in $C$ is nullhomotopic relative to
$p$. To construct the homotopy, first move the zero-cells to $p$ along
paths. Extend across one-cells using $\pi_1(C,p)=0$, and across
two-cells using $\pi_2(C,p)=0$. At each step the obstruction is the
map on the boundary of a cell times the homotopy interval, including
the two end disks, namely a sphere in the indicated homotopy group.
The weak topology pastes these extensions. This contracts the
\emph{inclusion} $A\to C$; it does not assert that $A$ is contractible.

By the homotopy extension property, extend this homotopy over $C$.
At its endpoint obtain a map $a:C\to C$, homotopic to the identity
relative to $p$, such that $a(A)=\{p\}$. For each characteristic map
$c_j:D^3\to C$ of a three-cell, $a c_j$ is constant on $\partial D^3$.
Surjectivity of $f_*$ gives a map
$g_j:(D^3,\partial D^3)\to(S^3,*)$ and a homotopy
$f g_j\simeq a c_j$ relative to the boundary.

Paste the maps $g_j$ with the constant map on $A$ to obtain $g:C\to S^3$.
Paste the relative homotopies with the constant homotopy on $A$.
Their boundaries agree, and the weak topology gives continuity, so
$fg\simeq a\simeq\operatorname{id}_C$ relative to $p$.
Consequently $fgf\simeq f$ as based maps. Injectivity of $f_*$ now
implies $gf\simeq\operatorname{id}_{S^3}$: precompose with
$I^3/\partial I^3\cong S^3$ to regard the two maps as elements of
$\pi_3(S^3,*)$, cancel $f_*$, and descend the resulting relative
homotopy to the quotient. Thus $f$ and $g$ are homotopy inverses.
\end{proof}

Apply the proposition to the CW structure of
Proposition~\ref{prop:foundations-triangulation}, using
Corollary~\ref{cor:foundations-homotopy-groups}. Together with
Lemma~\ref{lem:foundations-determinant-cover}, this finishes the proof
of Proposition~\ref{prop:foundations-initial}. Later width arguments
need a nonzero class, not this stronger unbased equivalence.

The stronger conclusion nevertheless has separate uses in transporting
topology through surgery. For example, if $e:X\simeq S^3$ and
$u:S^2\to X$ is continuous, then $eu$ is nullhomotopic because
$\pi_2(S^3)=0$. Composing a nullhomotopy with a homotopy inverse of $e$
and using its inverse homotopy gives a free nullhomotopy of $u$.
Also, for homotopy three-spheres $X,Y$, conjugating a particular map
$f:X\to Y$ by chosen sphere equivalences gives a self-map of $S^3$.
If $f_*$ is an isomorphism on $H_3$, the conjugate has degree $1$ or
$-1$. The degree classification of sphere maps
\cite[Corollary 4.25, p. 361]{Hatcher} then supplies an inverse
up to homotopy. The surgery comparison chapter must prove that its
\emph{selected} comparison map has this homology property and retain
that map as the forward map of the resulting equivalence. Merely knowing
that some equivalence between $X$ and $Y$ exists would not suffice.

\section{Excluding a two-sided projective plane}
\label{sec:foundations-projective-plane}

\begin{definition}[The excluded neighborhood]
\label{def:foundations-projective-neighborhood}
Let $\mathbb{RP}^2=S^2/(u\sim-u)$ and let $J=(-1,1)$. A trivial
normal product neighborhood in a three-manifold $X$ is a topological
open embedding
\[
 j:\mathbb{RP}^2\times J\longrightarrow X.
\]
An open embedding is a homeomorphism onto an open subset of $X$.
We say that $X$ excludes such neighborhoods if no such $j$ exists.
\end{definition}

This is the precise form of the two-sided-projective-plane exclusion
needed below. It is a statement about the whole product neighborhood,
and does not assume that $j$ is differentiable.

\begin{proposition}[Orientation exclusion]
\label{prop:foundations-projective-exclusion}
A Hausdorff, second-countable smooth three-manifold with compatible
chart signs excludes the neighborhood of
Definition~\ref{def:foundations-projective-neighborhood}.
Compactness and simple connectedness are not needed in this statement.
\source{MT2007}\dcref{Theorem 0.3, footnote 2, p. xii}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First turn the signed atlas into an atlas with positive transition
determinants. Restrict each chart to the open sets where its sign is
constant and, on the negative part, compose with a fixed coordinate
reflection. The determinant sign in
Definition~\ref{def:foundations-chart-signs} says exactly that all the
new transition determinants are positive.

Suppose $j$ exists. Restrict these charts to its open image and pull
them back through $j$. Transitions in the pulled-back atlas are
restrictions of the original transitions: $j$ and $j^{-1}$ cancel.
Thus this produces a positive smooth atlas on
$\mathbb{RP}^2\times J$, even though $j$ was only a topological open
embedding. No derivative of $j$ has been used.

A positive atlas determines an integral local orientation. To verify
the needed sign assertion, let a transition fixing the origin have
derivative $A$. On a sufficiently small sphere, its remainder has
norm smaller than the lower bound for $\|Ax\|$. Straight-line
interpolation to $A$ therefore never meets the puncture, and induces
the same map on local homology. A linear map of positive determinant
is connected to the identity among invertible positive-determinant
maps, so it preserves the local generator. The coordinate generators
therefore agree on overlaps and define an orientation $O$ of the product.

Consider the double covering
\[
 q:S^2\times J\longrightarrow\mathbb{RP}^2\times J,
 \qquad q(u,t)=([u],t),
\]
with deck transformation $\tau(u,t)=(-u,t)$.
Pullback by the local homeomorphism $q$ gives a local orientation $Q$
of $S^2\times J$. Since $q\tau=q$, this orientation is invariant
under $\tau$.

There is also a standard orientation on the cover: use the open
embedding
\[
 \Phi:S^2\times(-1,1)\longrightarrow\mathbb R^3,
 \qquad \Phi(u,t)=(2+t)u.
\]
Its image is the shell $1<\|x\|<3$, and
$\Phi\tau=-\Phi$. The antipodal map of $\mathbb R^3$ reverses
local integral orientation. One can compute the sign without assigning
a smooth structure to the quotient. A reflection interchanges the
two open sets obtained from $S^2$ by deleting opposite poles. Its
map on their intersection is homotopic to the identity, while
interchanging the two sets negates the Mayer--Vietoris connecting map.
Hence reflection acts by $-1$ on $H_2(S^2)$. Radial retraction of
$\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}$ and the boundary isomorphism of its
relative pair transfer that sign to local $H_3$ at the origin.
Negation is the product of a reflection and a positive-determinant
linear map, so has the same sign. Translation transfers the calculation
to every point. This is the local version of
\cite[degree properties (e)--(f), p. 134]{Hatcher}.

Let $O_0$ be the resulting standard orientation of the connected shell.
Any local orientation $Q$ differs from $O_0$ by a locally constant sign.
Connectedness of $S^2\times J$ makes that sign a single number
$a\in\{1,-1\}$. But then
\[
 \tau^*Q=a\tau^*O_0=-aO_0=-Q,
\]
contradicting $\tau^*Q=Q$. A generator of an infinite cyclic local
homology group is not its own negative. Therefore $j$ cannot exist.
\end{proof}

The covering and the local-orientation interpretation are also described
in \cite[Example 1.43, p. 74; Section 3.3, pp. 233--235 and
Exercises 3--5, p. 257]{Hatcher}. Applying the proposition with
Lemma~\ref{lem:foundations-determinant-cover} gives the exclusion for
our original $M$. It is this product-neighborhood obstruction that is
passed to the surgery construction, as in
\cite[Theorem 0.3, footnote 2, p. xii]{MT2007}.

\section{The forward geometric and backward topological arguments}
\label{sec:foundations-route}

The remaining chapters use the preceding results in a fixed order.
Starting with the original smooth $M$, choose and normalize an initial
Riemannian metric. Its orientation and the exclusion of
Proposition~\ref{prop:foundations-projective-exclusion} enter the
global surgery-flow construction. The geometric chapters construct
one such flow, its slices $M_t$, and its surgery events, with a specified
identification of $M$ with the time-zero slice.

The homotopy input has a different role. Choose $p\in M$ and a nonzero
class in $\pi_3(M,p)$. The filling-width chapter must turn this into a
nontrivial family in the actual $C^1$ free-loop space. This requires
the based/free comparison, its chosen model sphere, and the transport
through the time-zero identification. None of those assertions follows
merely from writing $\pi_3(M)\cong\mathbb Z$. The geometric estimates
then control that family's width through ordinary flow intervals and
surgery events. The extinction argument applies these estimates to
the same fixed flow and proves that it becomes empty in finite time.

The basepoint part of this interface can be stated explicitly. Suppose
the topology construction chose $p$ and the selected initial component
uses $q$. Choose a path $\gamma:p\to q$ and let $B_k(\gamma)$ be the
based transport on $\pi_k$. The reverse-path law makes this map an
isomorphism preserving zero. If $\xi_p$ corresponds to $1\in\mathbb Z$,
then $\xi_q=B_3(\gamma)\xi_p$ is nonzero, and $B_2(\gamma)$ transfers
the vanishing of $\pi_2$ to $q$. The loop-space comparison at $q$,
written $\Phi_{M,q}:\pi_2(\Lambda^1 M,\operatorname{const}_q)
\to\pi_3(M,q)$, then defines
$\alpha=\Phi_{M,q}^{-1}(\xi_q)\ne0$.
Here $\Lambda^1M$ denotes the $C^1$ free-loop space with the topology
specified in the filling-width chapter. Construction of $\Phi_{M,q}$ and
of regular representative families belongs to that chapter; this
argument identifies the exact class it must represent. For the flow
application, perform this construction on its fixed initial component
model $C_0$, after transporting the manifold hypotheses through the
specified time-zero identification. The point $q$ is the selected point
of $C_0$, and its metric is the pullback of the actual time-zero metric.
Section~\ref{sec:filling-fixed-initial-class} fixes these choices,
the sphere quotient, and the loop identification before any terminal
time or target component is chosen.

The proof then runs backward through the topological history of that
flow. Every event records its pre-surgery and post-surgery components,
its discarded pieces, and the balls and collars used in reconstruction.
Finite-history induction replaces the outgoing components by their
already reconstructed assemblies. Simple connectedness of the initial
manifold is used with the actual connected-sum group retractions to
restrict the possible factors. The sphere-reduction argument cancels
the resulting sphere factors using the recorded attaching data and
produces a diffeomorphism of the original $M$ with $S^3$.
These reconstruction statements, rather than the homotopy equivalence
of Section~\ref{sec:foundations-cw-equivalence}, give the smooth endpoint.

Finally, for a topological $M$, the smoothing chapters must construct a
smooth model $N$ and one specified homeomorphism $h:M\to N$.
Transport compactness, separation, countability, and simple connectedness
through this $h$. The smooth theorem gives a diffeomorphism
$d:N\to S^3$, and the composite $d\circ h$ is the required
homeomorphism. All topology established earlier is applied to this
same smooth model; an unspecified smoothing is not a substitute for
the required compatible model and its homeomorphism.

The substantive inputs promised by the later chapters are therefore:
existence and calibration of the surgery flow; the based/free family
comparison and width inequalities; finite extinction of that flow;
reconstruction and sphere reduction of its finite history; and compatible
smoothing. This chapter supplies the initial topology and orientation
obstruction, while keeping those later obligations distinct.
